How long would it take a competitor to reproduce what took your company years to build?

Artificial intelligence is shortening that timeline. When a tool can produce a credible alternative in an hour, the intellectual property behind a project may lose value sooner than expected. Within days, a competitor could develop an improved version of a product that once seemed protected by its complexity, development costs or the team's accumulated experience.

This does not mean innovation no longer matters. It means that the difficulty of building a product no longer guarantees that it will retain its value or sustain a lasting competitive advantage. For business leaders, the question is strategic: which assets will continue to set the company apart when solutions can be imitated quickly?

## When the product stops being a moat

For a long time, the value of a technology company was associated with what it had created: its product, code, design, patent or platform. These were valuable assets because reproducing them required time, investment and specialised knowledge.

AI is changing that equation. It makes it easier to analyse, imitate and recombine existing solutions. The result does not have to be identical; it only needs to solve the same problem more cheaply, quickly or conveniently to challenge the value of the original.

In some markets, that pressure can rapidly erode an advantage that once seemed to underpin a multimillion-dollar valuation. Intellectual property still matters, but its ability to act as a barrier may last less time than many companies expect.

## Two assets that are more resilient

In this environment, two assets can retain their value even when a product becomes replicable.

The first is trust: customers continuing to choose a brand even when a functional alternative exists. This is about more than having “fans”; it means building a relationship and reputation through consistent experiences. A strong brand can give a company time to adapt, provided it delivers on its promise.

The second is *know-how*: the knowledge that enables sound decisions in real-world situations. It includes what is written in manuals, but also the judgement, exceptions, early warning signs, lessons learned and understanding of how customers, materials, processes and systems behave.

This knowledge may be distributed across people, documents, conversations, tools and routines. If it remains fragmented or inaccessible, it is part of the company's assets but cannot inform its decisions or drive its growth.

## The context paradox

Here is an uncomfortable paradox for any leadership team: sometimes an AI system appears to have more context about the company than the organisation itself.

Not because it has more experience, but because it can access information that is better organised and easier to search than what the company has managed to bring together internally. An assistant connected to documents may find an answer that no one can readily locate. It may also produce a persuasive conclusion that is incomplete, out of date or wrong if the sources lack owners and controls.

AI does not automatically turn data into knowledge. If sources are duplicated, it is unclear which one is authoritative, or the reasons behind a decision are unknown, the system can amplify disorder and present its output with confidence. Useful context is not about accumulating files: it requires known provenance, assured quality, defined permissions, regular updates and clear ownership.

## Data governance is knowledge governance

That is why Data Governance is not a filing project or the sole responsibility of the technology department. It is a strategic capability: identifying available information, assigning owners, agreeing on what it means, establishing who can use it and ensuring that it remains reliable.

The starting point is not to catalogue everything. Instead, select a process where knowledge has a clear business impact and ask practical questions:

- Which critical decisions depend on the experience of just a few people?
- Which sources are reliable, and who is responsible for keeping them current?
- Which criteria and exceptions are not documented?
- What information can be used with AI, and what access restrictions must apply?
- How will the usefulness of the answers be evaluated, and who will correct errors?

The answers can turn tacit experience into useful context without pretending everything can be automated. They also help protect sensitive information and determine when a decision requires human judgement.

## An advantage you cannot download

The best-positioned company will not necessarily be the one with the most data or the latest model. It will be the one that identifies its distinctive knowledge, preserves its provenance and effectively incorporates it into decisions and operations.

Products will remain important, as will innovation, execution and customer relationships. But as replicating a solution becomes commonplace, a growing share of competitive advantage will come from combining trust with proprietary knowledge: the context accumulated through years of decisions, which cannot be reproduced with a simple prompt.

For company leaders, the question is no longer only what the business has built. It is also whether the organisation can identify and demonstrate the value of its knowledge—and turn it into a competitive advantage.
