AliExpress and Temu are often described as online shops for clothes, accessories and inexpensive odds and ends. That description is incomplete. For makers, students, small companies and independent engineers, they are also global catalogues of electronic components, tools, mechanical parts and development boards. An imperfect but often essential source of materials for learning, prototyping and solving problems that local distributors do not always cover competitively.

A robot can begin with a simple idea, a worktable and a budget of €150. A couple of microcontrollers, a few motors, sensors, resistors and a battery are enough to start testing. Not to manufacture at scale, but to find out what works and what does not. Before a product reaches a factory, it often passes through a desk where someone must be able to afford the first experiment.

Since July 2026, purchases in small consignments from outside the European Union have been subject to a temporary €3 duty for consignments worth up to €150. Since November, a €2 handling fee per item has also been added. The change affects purchases on platforms such as AliExpress and Temu, and makes a question that European industrial policy should not overlook even more pressing: what happens to people who need to import parts because they still cannot find an affordable local alternative?

## Importing a product is not the same as importing a possibility

Brussels presents these measures as a way to respond to the volume of imports, improve oversight and protect European retailers. Those are legitimate aims. So is requiring products to meet safety standards and ensuring that sellers compete under clear rules.

The problem is that discussions often conflate two different things: buying a finished product and buying components to create something new. Someone ordering two ESP32 boards and four motors is not necessarily replacing an equivalent European purchase. They may be acquiring the means to learn robotics, design a mechanism or build a first prototype. In this context, the platform is not merely where something is bought; it is part of the supply chain of experimentation.

European distributors offer real advantages: faster availability, traceability, documentation and warranties. For many professional projects, those differences justify the price. But for someone who needs only a few units, the cost can be decisive, especially when there is no comparable local alternative.

**Innovation begins when you can afford to make mistakes.**

A low-cost motor lets you test a movement. A sensor helps you check a hypothesis. A development board may end up in a drawer after an afternoon of experiments; it may also teach you enough to make the next design better. If every attempt costs several times more, there will be fewer attempts and less hands-on learning.

## Resilience is learned, too

In March 2020, during the health emergency, many people put their skills and equipment at the service of hospitals. The Coronavirus Makers initiative brought together more than 13,500 volunteers in ten days and coordinated designs and distributed manufacturing. It did not replace the medical industry, but it showed the value of having people able to design, adapt and produce when conventional responses were not enough.

The lesson is not that a maker community can replace an industrial supply chain. It is that skills, tools and collaborative networks expand a society's ability to respond. In the face of a logistics disruption, an energy crisis or a supply restriction, knowing how to repair and adapt equipment can matter as much as having access to it.

That capacity does not appear overnight. It develops in workshops, universities, laboratories, small businesses and garages. And it develops by doing: testing components, reading documentation, making mistakes and sharing what you learn.

## The industrial contradiction

Europe talks about technological sovereignty, reindustrialisation and strategic autonomy. Spain wants more technology companies and more domestic manufacturing. Yet if people who could become future manufacturers find it more expensive to access parts that are not yet produced or distributed competitively here, there is a contradiction worth examining.

This is not about defending any particular platform or denying the problems associated with imports. It is about recognising that, in many categories, international marketplaces meet a need that European suppliers still do not serve competitively for small orders. Treating every shipment as if it contained a finished consumer product misses the difference between buying another object and acquiring the means to build one.

A strong industrial policy should work on both fronts: proportionate controls and obligations for imported products, and the conditions needed to make and distribute alternatives here. That means supporting production capacity, enabling access to components and improving the offer for people buying small quantities. Barriers alone do not create manufacturers.

**Technological sovereignty is not about making it harder to buy from abroad. It is about making sure that one day we can manufacture here.**

## Don't make getting started a luxury

Perhaps Spain's next robotics project is taking shape right now on a student's desk. Its creator does not need a thousand motors; they need four. They do not need a production line; they need a prototype they can assemble, test and improve.

Before a company, there is an idea. Before a factory, there is a first design. And before an industry exists, someone has to be able to afford to get started.

Protecting retailers and consumer safety matters. So does avoiding unnecessary costs on the learning and experimentation that can help build the industry we still lack.

**Let's not make the road to the industry Spain needs to build more expensive.**
