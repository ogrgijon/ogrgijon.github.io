## Building is about relationships

Can a building be shelter, machine, symbol and work of art at the same time? *De architectura*, the ten-book treatise attributed to Vitruvius, begins with that ambition. Its pages explore materials, techniques, cities, temples, proportions and machines, but its deeper subject is how knowledge can become places capable of sustaining human life.

Vitruvius writes in the age of Augustus, when imperial power was transforming cities and architecture was also becoming a political statement. He offers more than instructions: he argues for a culture of design in which builders must observe the world, understand mathematics, listen to place and exercise judgment responsibly.

His famous triad—*firmitas*, *utilitas* and *venustas*: durability, usefulness and beauty—still invites us to bring together what is often separated. A building must stand, serve those who inhabit it and offer a meaningful experience. Vitruvius' philosophy is found here: beauty is not an ornament added to technique, but a way of ordering the relationship between matter, use and proportion.

### From the treatise to our cities

Vitruvius writes from an ancient context, and his ideas are not a universal manual for designing today. That is precisely what makes them so stimulating. Read critically, they open questions that remain alive: Who is a building for? What kinds of knowledge does a designer need? How do place, materials and use relate to one another? The treatise reminds us that every building contains an idea of society.

**To think further:** the public responsibility of design, and the possibility of measuring a space not only by its appearance, but by the dignity, orientation and possibilities for life it offers.
